## Exact practice prompts and verified keys

### Level 1: Find the Subject and Verb

The printed directions ask for subject and verb and permit `missing` when either is absent. The key identifies simple subjects (head nouns), so accept the head noun and explain any highlighted full subject separately.

| # | Exact teacher word group | Teacher key / implementation treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The puppy barked. | Subject `puppy`; verb `barked`. |
| 2 | Across the busy playground | Subject missing; verb missing. `playground` is a noun inside a prepositional phrase, not a clause subject here. |
| 3 | Running quickly toward the bus | Subject missing; **verb form `running`**. The key expressly uses “verb form,” not a complete main verb. |
| 4 | The old train rumbled. | Subject `train`; verb `rumbled`. |

Source worked examples: `The small bird chirped.` → subject `bird`, verb `chirped`; `Under the tall bridge` → subject missing, verb missing. Do not silently substitute the brief's illustrative puppy example for these source examples when labeling an activity as a teacher worked example.

**Important Level 1 #3 design decision:** A field labeled “Verb or verb form” should accept `running`, matching the key. A separate field explicitly asking for a “complete main verb” may accept “No complete main verb,” but the explanation must still identify `running` as a verb form. Do not label `running` a non-verb or mark the teacher's answer wrong. A picture of a runner supplies context only; it does not add a written subject to the word group.

### Level 2: Phrase or Clause?

Source direction: use P for phrase or C for clause. The table omits only the answer-blank colon.

| # | Exact teacher word group | Key | Reason to teach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Before dinner | P | No subject–verb clause pair. |
| 2 | Before dinner was served | C | `dinner` + `was served`; dependent, but still a clause. |
| 3 | The guests arrived | C | `guests` + `arrived`. |
| 4 | Running quickly through the park | P | `Running` is a verb form; no stated subject and complete main verb pair in this classroom classification. |
| 5 | The runners were moving quickly | C | `runners` + the full verb phrase `were moving`. |
| 6 | After the long practice | P | No clause pair; `practice` is a noun here. |
| 7 | After practice ended | C | `practice` + `ended`; dependent, but still a clause. |
| 8 | Covered in fresh snow | P | `Covered` is a participial verb form, not a complete main verb in this group. |

Source worked examples: `Near the window` → P; `The bird sang` → C (`bird`, `sang`). These groups do not have terminal periods in the examples.

### Level 3: Independent or Dependent Clause?

Source direction: use I for independent clause or D for dependent clause. Source reminders distinguish a self-sufficient subject–verb thought from one needing another clause. Worked examples are `Because the rain stopped` → D and `The rain stopped` → I.

| # | Exact teacher word group | Printed key | Correct scope and treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Although the trail was steep | D | Whole word group is a dependent clause (`trail`, `was`). |
| 2 | The hikers rested | I | Whole word group is an independent clause (`hikers`, `rested`). |
| 3 | When the bell rang | D | Whole word group is dependent (`bell`, `rang`). |
| 4 | Students packed their books | I | Whole word group is independent (`Students`, `packed`). |
| 5 | If the library is open | D | Whole word group is dependent (`library`, `is`). |
| 6 | We will return tomorrow | I | Whole word group is independent (`We`, full verb `will return`). |
| 7 | The artist who painted the mural | D | The key's explanation refers specifically to `who painted the mural`, a dependent relative clause. The **entire** group is a noun phrase containing that clause, with no main predicate for `The artist`. |
| 8 | I found the book that you recommended | D | The key's explanation refers specifically to `that you recommended`, a dependent relative clause. The **entire** group is a complete complex sentence, containing the independent main-clause frame `I found the book`. |

**Required visible clarification for #7/#8:** Keep the full teacher word group on screen. Clearly bracket/highlight the exact relative clause and explicitly ask the learner to classify **those highlighted words**. The printed D then applies to the same embedded clause discussed by the key. After answering, explain the whole-group structure in a short separate sentence. If a question instead asks about all of #7, the correct description is “noun phrase / fragment containing a dependent clause,” not simply dependent clause. If it asks whether all of #8 can stand as a sentence, the answer is yes. Never score the full #8 as dependent merely because it contains `that`.

In `who painted the mural`, `who` acts as the relative clause's subject and `painted` its verb. In `that you recommended`, `you` is the subject and `recommended` is the verb; `that` connects to the book as the verb's object. A rule that every `who` or `that` is a subject would misteach #8.

### Level 4: Fix and Combine

Subheading: **Rewrite the Fragments**. The directions permit adding an independent clause or removing the word that creates dependence. All three typed prompts retain their original final period.

| # | Exact teacher fragment | Printed possible answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Before the movie began. | Before the movie began, we bought popcorn. |
| 2 | Running quickly down the sidewalk. | The child was running quickly down the sidewalk. |
| 3 | Although the test was difficult. | Although the test was difficult, I did my best. |

Source worked example: `Because the power went out.` → `Because the power went out, we used flashlights.`

The key explicitly allows varied answers. Accept valid rewrites such as `The movie began.` and `The test was difficult.` as well as expansions, because removing the dependency marker is authorized. `The child running quickly down the sidewalk.` remains a fragment; `The child was running quickly down the sidewalk.` supplies a complete main verb. These are illustrative checks, not a safe general-purpose grammar algorithm.

Use typed attempts followed by a model and self-check, rather than exact-string grading. Keep these responses separate from the automatically scored test items. Do not expose a model before the learner submits an attempt. A checklist should cover subject, complete main verb, complete thought, joining a dependent clause, and suitable punctuation. Passing a checklist is learner self-check, not a claim of automated grammar validation.

## Primary grammar verification

The following primary educational sources support the decisions above. Page text is paraphrased; none of their example sentences is copied into the lesson.

1. [Purdue OWL: Identifying Independent and Dependent Clauses](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/independent_and_dependent_clauses/index.html) supports the classroom distinction between complete and dependent subject–verb thoughts, joining dependent and independent clauses, and fixing fragments by joining or removing a dependency marker.
2. [Purdue OWL: Sentence Fragments](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/sentence_fragments.html) explains that a fragment may lack a subject or a complete main verb. This supports explaining why a visible action-like word is insufficient in the runner items.
3. [Purdue OWL: Participles](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/gerunds_participles_and_infinitives/participles.html) supports the traditional classroom treatment of participial phrases and the distinction between a verbal form and the complete main predicate.
4. [Purdue OWL: Introduction and General Usage in Defining Clauses](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/grammar/relative_pronouns/index.html) identifies relative clauses as dependent structures modifying another element. This is the basis for separating #7/#8's embedded clauses from their enclosing groups.
5. [Cambridge Dictionary, English Grammar Today: Relative pronouns](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/pronouns-relative) supports identifying a relative pronoun's role as subject or object, rather than classifying by its presence alone.
6. [Cambridge Dictionary, English Grammar Today: Clauses, finite and non-finite](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/grammar/british-grammar/clauses-finite-and-non-finite) describes a broader grammar framework that can call some subjectless participial structures non-finite clauses. The module intentionally uses the teacher's traditional **phrase** label for the supplied runner/snow items; it should not claim this simplified classification exhausts every grammatical framework.

Cambridge's directly opened pages returned HTTP 403 to the browsing tool, but the primary-domain indexed results provided the relevant page text. Purdue pages were read directly. Grammar conclusions about the particular teacher sentences are application of these rules, not claimed quotations from either publisher.
