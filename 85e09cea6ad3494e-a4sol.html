Complementary Goods Pricing: GrillKing & BunCraft (Q1–Q3)

Setup: GrillKing (hot dogs) and BunCraft (buns) are complementary products sold in 6-packs. Estimated weekly demand: SalesGK = 500 − 30 · PriceGK − 10 · PriceBC
SalesBC = 450 − 28 · PriceBC − 8 · PriceGK Production costs: $3.00/6-pack (GrillKing), $2.00/6-pack (BunCraft).

Tool: Use Tool 11: Competitive & Channel Pricing. Under the Manual Parameters tab, enter: Product 1 (GrillKing):   a1 = 500,   b1 = −30,   C1 = 3
Product 2 (BunCraft):   a2 = 450,   b2 = −28,   C2 = 2
Cross-Price Effects:   c1 (P2 → Q1) = −10,   c2 (P1 → Q2) = −8
Question 13 pts
Calculate predicted sales of hot dogs when prices are $6 and $4, for hot dogs and buns, respectively.

Calculation

SalesGK = 500 − 30(6) − 10(4) = 500 − 180 − 40 = 280

Using Tool 11

Under Demand Prediction, enter P1 = 6 and P2 = 4, then click Predict Demand. The tool returns Q1 = 280.

✓ Answer: 280
Question 23 pts
What is the calculated cross-price elasticity of demand for BunCraft when the price of GrillKing increases from $9 to $10 when the price of BunCraft is $10?

Using Tool 11

Under the Elasticity Calculator, select Vary P1 (hold P2 constant), then enter P1 (initial) = 9, P1 (new) = 10, P2 (held constant) = 10. Click Calculate Elasticities. The tool returns a cross-price elasticity of Q2 (w.r.t. P1) = −0.7347.

Manual verification

SalesBC at PGK=9: 450 − 28(10) − 8(9) = 98

SalesBC at PGK=10: 450 − 28(10) − 8(10) = 90

%ΔQ = (90−98)/98 = −8.16%,   %ΔP = (10−9)/9 = 11.11%

Cross-price elasticity = −8.16% / 11.11% = −0.7347

The negative sign confirms the products are complements: when hot dog prices rise, bun sales fall.

✓ Answer: −0.7347

3.1 — Complement Pricing Externality

When GrillKing and BunCraft are separate firms, each sets its price to maximize its own profit without considering that a higher price for one product reduces demand for the other. Since the products are complements, raising the hot dog price hurts bun sales (and vice versa). The single owner internalizes this negative externality and sets lower prices on both products (PGK = $8.00 vs. $8.53, PBC = $7.00 vs. $7.82), leading to higher combined demand and higher total profit ($1,900 vs. $1,865) despite lower individual margins.

3.2 — Double Marginalization

When the single owner sells through a retailer, two separate markups are stacked: the owner marks up above production cost when setting wholesale prices ($7.75 and $7.25), and the retailer marks up again when setting retail prices ($10.25 and $9.75). This double marginalization raises the final price to consumers well above the directly optimal level, cutting sales in half (190 → 95) and reducing total channel profit by $475 ($1,900 → $1,425). The retailer captures $475 of this reduced pie, but the overall pie shrinks.

3.3 — Two Simultaneous Inefficiencies

This scenario combines both inefficiencies: (1) the horizontal complement pricing externality (each competitor ignores the cross-product demand effect, pushing wholesale prices to $8.44 and $7.90) and (2) vertical double marginalization (the retailer adds another markup, pushing retail prices to $10.58 and $10.09). Their combined effect is worse than the sum of their individual effects because they compound: the horizontal externality inflates wholesale prices, and the retailer’s markup amplifies these already-inflated prices further, producing the highest retail prices and the lowest total channel profit ($1,289).

3.4 — Consumer Ranking

From best to worst for consumers (lowest to highest retail prices):

(1) Single Owner, Direct Sales (PGK = $8.00, PBC = $7.00)

(2) Competitors, Direct Sales (PGK = $8.53, PBC = $7.82)

(3) Single Owner + Retailer (PGK = $10.25, PBC = $9.75)

(4) Competitors + Retailer (PGK = $10.58, PBC = $10.09)

The key insight is that the vertical inefficiency (double marginalization from adding a retailer) raises prices for consumers more than the horizontal complement externality (having separate competitors instead of a single owner). If the products were substitutes instead of complements, the ranking would change: competition between substitute producers drives prices down, so the competitor-direct scenario would produce the lowest prices and become the best outcome for consumers.

Log-Log Demand Model (Q4–Q6)

Model: ln(Sales) = 1 − 2 · ln(Price) + 0.3 · Summer Sales in units, Price in dollars, Summer is a dummy (1 if summer). All estimates significant at 5%.
Question 43 pts · True / False
During a non-summer season, when the price is set to zero, the predicted unit sales become exp{1}.

FALSE. To interpret the intercept, we need the intercept to stand alone on the right-hand side of the equation. That happens when all other terms equal zero: ln(Price) = 0 and Summer = 0. Since ln(Price) = 0 when Price = 1 (not when Price = 0), the intercept is isolated at Price = $1 in a non-summer season. At that point, ln(Sales) = 1, and to get Sales we take the exponential: Sales = exp(1). The statement is false because it says this occurs when Price = 0, but ln(0) is undefined — you cannot set Price to zero in a log-log model.

✓ Answer: FALSE
Question 54 pts · True / False
An increase in price from $100 to $101 increases unit sales by about 2%.

FALSE. Two problems. First, the coefficient on ln(Price) is −2, which in a log-log model is the price elasticity. A 1% increase in price leads to approximately a 2% decrease in sales, not an increase — the statement gets the direction wrong. Second, the statement is incomplete: the interpretation must include “holding the season constant,” since the model includes Summer as a second independent variable.

✓ Answer: FALSE
Question 65 pts
What is the predicted difference in sales between a summer and a non-summer season if the price is $1? (Round to closest integer.)

Calculation

At Price = $1: ln(1) = 0

Summer: ln(Sales) = 1 − 2(0) + 0.3(1) = 1.3  →  Sales = exp(1.3) = 3.669

Non-summer: ln(Sales) = 1 − 2(0) + 0.3(0) = 1.0  →  Sales = exp(1.0) = 2.718

Difference = 3.669 − 2.718 = 0.951 ≈ 1

✓ Answer: 1

Log-Linear Model & Pass-Through (Q7–Q8)

Model: ln(Sales) = 16 − 5 · Retail_Price Weekly store-level ice cream sales. Wholesale price = $1/unit. Kroger sets retail price to maximize profit.
Question 75 pts
The manufacturer cuts the wholesale price by 20% (from $1.00 to $0.80). What retail price discount (in cents) should Kroger offer?

Step 1 — Find the original optimal price using Tool 8: General Purpose Optimizer

Enter the equations below. Use Retail_Price as the decision variable between 0.80 and 20. Leave Control Variables empty.

Sales = exp(16 - 5 * Retail_Price)
Profit = (Retail_Price - 1) * Sales

The tool returns Retail_Price = $1.20.

Step 2 — Find the new optimal price after the wholesale cut

Change the Profit equation to reflect the new cost of $0.80:

Sales = exp(16 - 5 * Retail_Price)
Profit = (Retail_Price - 0.8) * Sales

The tool returns Retail_Price = $1.00.

Discount

$1.20 − $1.00 = 20 cents

✓ Answer: 20 cents
Question 85 pts
Instead of cutting wholesale price, the manufacturer provides a 20-cent coupon directly to consumers (100% redemption). What retail price discount (in cents) should Kroger offer?

Key Difference from Q7

The coupon reduces the consumer’s net price, not Kroger’s cost. Without the coupon, consumers pay P and demand is Sales = exp(16 − 5 * P). With the coupon, consumers effectively pay (P − 0.20), so demand becomes Sales = exp(16 − 5 * (P − 0.20)). Kroger’s cost is still $1.

Using Tool 8: General Purpose Optimizer

The only thing that changes from Q7 is the Sales equation. The Profit equation stays the same (cost is still $1).

Sales = exp(16 - 5 * (Retail_Price - 0.20))
Profit = (Retail_Price - 1) * Sales

The tool returns Retail_Price = $1.20 — the same as the original (no coupon) case. Kroger does not change its price.

Discount

$1.20 − $1.20 = 0 cents

✓ Answer: 0 cents

Regression Interpretation (Q9–Q10)

Question 95 pts
In Y = β0 + β1X1 + β2X2, which can be said about β1?

(A) Slope of the first independent variable.
(B) Predicted value of the DV when the first IV is zero.
(C) The increase in the DV when the first IV increases by 1 unit.

A — TRUE. β1 is the slope on X1: the rate of change in Y per unit change in X1, holding X2 constant.

B — FALSE. When X1 = 0 alone, the predicted value is β0 + β2X2, not β1. The intercept β0 is the predicted value of Y when both X1 = 0 and X2 = 0.

C — FALSE. The statement says “the increase in the DV when the first IV increases by 1 unit” but does not specify that the second IV must be held constant. Without that condition, the statement is incomplete and therefore incorrect.

✓ Answer: A
