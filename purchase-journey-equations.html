FashionNext · M574

Consumer Purchase Journey

Aggregate Revenue vs. Consumer Journey Decomposition · Performance Driven Pricing

FashionNext is an online clothing retailer running weekly discount events to drive consumer visits and spending. A consumer typically makes multiple decisions on each trip — whether to visit the store, how many product categories to buy, and how much to spend in each category — and a single marketing lever like a discount can have different effects at each stage. We compare two approaches on the same 448-consumer × 25-week panel: (1) an aggregate benchmark that regresses total pre-discount revenue on the discount, and (2) a purchase-journey decomposition that models each stage separately and then recombines them. Each setup is shown in standard equation form, with code blocks ready to paste into Tool 8: General Purpose Optimizer to find the profit-maximizing discount.

Aggregate Benchmark

1 regression

Treat each consumer-week as a single aggregate outcome and regress pre-discount total revenue directly on the discount. This is the simplest approach and collapses the entire purchase decision into one equation.

Linear Regression Estimates

Coefficients (DV: Pre-Discount Total Revenue)

Estimated in Tool 7 by running a linear regression of per-consumer-week pre-discount total revenue on the discount level.

Term Estimate
Intercept 0.7121
Discount 0.3638
Pre-Discount Total Revenue

A single linear equation in the discount. The intercept is the expected pre-discount revenue per consumer-week at zero discount; the slope says each additional percentage point of discount raises pre-discount revenue by 0.3638.

$$\text{Pre-Discount Total Revenue} = 0.7121 + 0.3638 \cdot \text{Discount}$$
Profit

FashionNext's sticker prices embed a 1/3 gross margin, so the cost of goods is 2/3 of the pre-discount revenue. Subtracting the discount given back to the shopper yields: Profit = Pre-Discount Revenue × (1/3) − Pre-Discount Revenue × (Discount / 100).

$$\text{Profit} = \text{Pre-Discount Total Revenue} \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} \;-\; \text{Pre-Discount Total Revenue} \cdot \tfrac{\text{Discount}}{100}$$
Paste into Tool 8: General Purpose Optimizer (decision: Discount)
# Aggregate Benchmark
# Decision variable: Discount  (search range: 0 to 100)

Pre_Discount_Total_Revenue = 0.7121 + 0.3638 * Discount

Profit = Pre_Discount_Total_Revenue * (1/3) - Pre_Discount_Total_Revenue * (Discount / 100)

Purchase-Journey Decomposition

3 regressions

Instead of one aggregate regression, model each stage of the consumer trip separately — whether the consumer visits, how many categories they buy given a visit, and how much they spend per category given a purchase — and recombine the three pieces to get pre-discount total revenue.

Stage 1

Visit the Store

Binary Logit Regression
Visit Probability

A binary logistic regression estimates the probability that a consumer visits the store in a given week. The discount coefficient is positive: bigger discounts attract more store visits.

$$\Pr(\text{Visit}) = \frac{\exp(-4.4262 + 0.1374 \cdot \text{Discount})}{1 + \exp(-4.4262 + 0.1374 \cdot \text{Discount})}$$
Stage 2

Categories Purchased

Poisson Regression
Mean Category Count (conditional on visit)

A Poisson regression estimates the expected number of product categories a consumer buys, given that they visited. Because this is a conditional model, the panel is first filtered to rows where visit == 1 and the regression is estimated only on those observations. The discount coefficient is negative: with a discount in hand, consumers tend to focus on fewer categories (e.g., budget constraints, preplanned purchases).

$$E[\,\text{Category Count} \mid \text{Visit}\,] = \exp(0.4213 - 0.0153 \cdot \text{Discount})$$
Stage 3

Per-Category Spending

Linear Regression
Pre-Discount Per-Category Revenue (conditional on purchase)

A linear regression estimates pre-discount dollars spent per category, conditional on buying something in that category. Because this is a conditional model, the panel is first filtered to rows where numofcategories > 0 and the regression is estimated only on those observations. The discount coefficient is positive: once a consumer is buying, larger discounts lead them to spend more per category.

$$\text{Pre-Discount Per-Category Revenue} = 44.0025 + 0.3504 \cdot \text{Discount}$$

Recombining the Three Stages

Pre-Discount Total Revenue

Expected pre-discount total revenue is the product of the three stage components: visit probability × mean categories per visit × revenue per category.

$$\text{Pre-Discount Total Revenue} = \Pr(\text{Visit}) \cdot E[\,\text{Category Count} \mid \text{Visit}\,] \cdot \text{Pre-Discount Per-Category Revenue}$$
Profit

Same profit formula as the benchmark: 1/3 gross margin on pre-discount revenue, minus the discount given back to the shopper.

$$\text{Profit} = \text{Pre-Discount Total Revenue} \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} \;-\; \text{Pre-Discount Total Revenue} \cdot \tfrac{\text{Discount}}{100}$$
Paste into Tool 8: General Purpose Optimizer (decision: Discount)
# Purchase-Journey Decomposition
# Decision variable: Discount  (search range: 0 to 100)

# Stage 1 — Visit (binary logit)
Prob_Visit = exp(-4.4262 + 0.1374 * Discount) / (1 + exp(-4.4262 + 0.1374 * Discount))

# Stage 2 — Category count | visit (Poisson)
Mean_Category_Count = exp(0.4213 - 0.0153 * Discount)

# Stage 3 — Per-category revenue | purchase (linear)
Pre_Discount_Per_Category_Revenue = 44.0025 + 0.3504 * Discount

# Recombine the three stages
Pre_Discount_Total_Revenue = Prob_Visit * Mean_Category_Count * Pre_Discount_Per_Category_Revenue

# Profit (1/3 gross margin; discount given back to shopper)
Profit = Pre_Discount_Total_Revenue * (1/3) - Pre_Discount_Total_Revenue * (Discount / 100)

Discussion of the Benchmark vs. Full Model
  • The benchmark model has a very low R², making it unsuitable for price optimization.
  • While the benchmark indicates that discounts increase revenue, the relationship is more complex. Looking at the three stages separately, we find that:
    • Discounts attract consumers to the retailer (visit slope +0.1374) and increase spending conditional on a purchase (per-category spending slope +0.3504).
    • However, discounts also reduce the number of categories purchased (category-count slope −0.0153).
      • This may be due to budget and time constraints, encouraging consumers to focus on more expensive categories or preplanned purchases (e.g., waiting for the right price to buy a specific item).
  • The benchmark model suggests an optimal discount rate of 15.69%, which results in approximately 23% lower profit compared to the full model specification (0.9799 vs. 1.2684). The full model identifies an optimal discount rate of 23.14%, reflecting a more accurate and profitable pricing strategy.

Extending the Framework

What we built this semester

The three-stage decomposition shown here is a clean starting point. Several of the techniques we covered earlier this semester can be embedded inside it to make it richer and more realistic. For example, you can enrich your decision support system (DSS) by:

  • Consumer heterogeneity at each stage — observed heterogeneity (demographics, RFM, past purchase behavior) and unobserved heterogeneity (latent-class / mixture models) can be added to each of the three regressions. This lets visit, category-count, and spending responses vary across customer segments rather than assuming one average effect.
  • Channel and market effects — channel-level and market-level intercepts and discount slopes can be estimated separately, as in the Tailor & Co. case, so the optimal discount can differ by sales venue or geography.
  • Dynamic discounting — demand, supply, and competitive shifters (seasonality, inventory levels, competitor prices, promotional calendars) can be added as controls so the optimal discount moves over time rather than being static.
And Finally — Keep It Alive
  • Whatever form of the model you end up with, it is only as useful as the data behind it.
  • Re-estimate the stage equations regularly as new transaction data comes in.
  • Discount elasticities are not constants — the true parameters drift as:
    • consumers learn the retailer's promotional patterns,
    • competitors adjust their own pricing, and
    • preferences shift with macroeconomic conditions.
  • A decision support system built once and never refreshed starts drifting away from reality the moment it is deployed.
  • Budgeting for continuous re-estimation is what separates a working pricing system from a static snapshot.
