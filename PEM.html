How good is your organization at pricing?

Most people cannot answer that question with anything better than “pretty good” or “we could be better.” That is a problem, because pricing is the most powerful lever a firm has on profitability: raising price by one percent without losing customers typically lifts operating profit by eight to eleven percent. No other lever — not cost cutting, not selling more units — comes close.

This assessment gives you a way to measure it. You rate your organization on eleven statements across three areas — Strategy & Culture (does the organization take pricing seriously?), Execution & Process (can it actually implement pricing decisions?), and Analytics & Data (does it have the information needed to price well?). Each statement is scored from 1, strongly disagree, to 5, strongly agree, and carries a weight you can adjust. The result is a weighted score out of 5, plus a subtotal for each of the three areas.

Hover over or click the ⓘ beside any statement to see what it is really asking.

Setting the weights

Every statement carries a weight, and the weights must total 100%. Eleven statements cannot be weighted perfectly equally, so the defaults give slightly more to the factors that tend to matter most broadly — Knowledge of Value and Pricing Objectives among them.

Those are only defaults. Change them to match your situation. What matters most depends on your industry, your competitive position, and your company’s stage:

  • A commodity market with heavy price competition, where rivals watch each other closely — raise the weight on Knowledge of Competition.
  • Complex B2B solutions with long sales cycles, large deals, and real negotiation — raise the weight on Knowledge of Value, since your ability to quantify it decides whether you hold price.
  • A five-person startup without sophisticated systems yet — lower IT Support and put the weight on Strategy & Culture. Systems can come later.

The weights force a decision about what matters most. You cannot call everything equally important — that is not strategy. Strategy is choosing.

How the overall score is classified

The weighted score is a weighted average of your eleven ratings, reported on the same 1–5 scale. It falls into one of four bands:

  • Below 2.50 Needs Improvement. Pricing capability is a live constraint on profitability. Most decisions are being made without the structure or information to support them.
  • 2.50 – 3.49 Developing. Some capability exists but it is uneven. On a 1–5 scale 3 is neutral, so a score here means the organization is not yet reliably good at pricing.
  • 3.50 – 4.49 Strong Capability. Pricing is managed deliberately, with real process and real data behind it.
  • 4.50 and above World Class. Pricing is a genuine source of competitive advantage.

Read the pattern, not just the total. The three section subtotals matter more than the headline number, because they tell you where to act. Three patterns come up repeatedly:

  • High Strategy, low Analytics. Leadership cares about pricing, but decisions rest on judgment rather than evidence. Invest in measurement and data.
  • High Analytics, low Execution. The data is good but you cannot act on it — systems are slow, or the field does not follow through. Fix the process.
  • High Execution, low Strategy. You can change prices quickly but you are not sure what to change them to. Efficient but directionless; set clearer objectives.

You do not need to fix everything at once. Start with the largest gap. The assessment is also more useful repeated — have colleagues complete it separately and compare where you disagree, then run it again in six months to see which capabilities you have actually built.
